# How I prompt now

For a while my prompts leaned on adjectives: "world class," "god tier," "PhD-level." They didn't help. What changed my results was replacing praise with specifics: who it's for, how long, what to keep, and how I'll know it worked.

These are real prompts of mine from one week of work, rewritten.

| Before | After | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Audit what I say but make it world class god tier and honest." | "Edit this Upwork review for grammar and clarity. Keep it under 70 words, use only claims I wrote, and tell me what you changed." | A length, a source rule, and a request to show the edits |
| "Heavily critique The Revenue Audit… is that worth $1500 to them?" | "Who would buy a $1,500 HubSpot audit, and who shouldn't? Compare a team that just bought HubSpot with one that's used it for years." | Named the two buyers to compare |
| "How would a world class god tier landing page expert critique and re-do this testimonial section?" | "This testimonial sits just above my pricing. Critique it for a CEO skimming in five seconds: alignment with the page, length, and which objection it answers. Keep it under 300 pixels tall." | The reader, the time they'll spend, the job it does, and a size limit |
| "Write for me a world class god tier resume." | "Refine my honest resume for customer success roles. Use only claims in my draft, keep it to one page, and list anything you'd cut." | A source rule, a length, and what to show me |
| "Rewrite this in laymen's terms but like a chill marketing VP who you'd love to work with." | (No change needed.) | It already named the audience and the tone. |

The pattern in the "after" column:

1. **Who's reading,** and how much time they'll give it.
2. **How long** it should be.
3. **What's off limits:** claims I didn't make, jargon, a format.
4. **How I'll judge it,** so the answer can check itself.
